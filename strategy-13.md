# STRATEGY GUIDE — Scenario 13: "The Night Shift" (Hard)

*Game documentation for a fictional simulation. This guide describes strategy inside the scenario only. It is general information about how the simulated system works — not legal advice for any real case.*

**Path check:** The route below was verified by walking `scenario-13.json` programmatically — every choice named here exists at the named node, and the sequence reaches the named ending. End cash on this route: $156 of $165.

## 1. The win

**"Ending — The Plan Moves."** This is a genuine but bounded win. The plan is amended: parenting classes move to a 6 P.M. cohort, screens move to your evenings off, visits move at the term's turn — and you keep the warehouse job and the apartment. State the limits honestly: the early "fatigued; engagement unclear" notes are **not** erased; they remain in the file as its first chapter. Three bridge weeks at the old 9:30 schedule still have to be survived. What you win is a plan scheduled against your actual life, and a record that explains itself in date order.

(The grind branch instead produces the closed-window missed screen; the quit math branch is analysis, not action.)

7. **Paper for the Review** → **The Review — The Plan Moves** — The six-document folder (plan p.9, employer letter, filed request, provider's answer, evening roster, interim notice) goes into the record; the judge confirms the amended plan. Reaches **Ending — The Plan Moves**.

## 3. Why this wins — the law as it is supposed to work

- **The record is available on written request** (55 Pa. Code § 3130.44(d)). Here it does the decisive work: the contradiction that powers the whole case — the plan requiring full-time employment and 9:30 A.M. presence in the same hours — is provable only from the *filed* plan, page nine. The summary you were handed never shows it.
- **Counsel turns a complaint into a filing** (42 Pa.C.S. § 6337). The lawyer's contributions in this scenario are small and mechanical: the request is written as a request, the employer letter is attached, a copy is kept. That is what makes it a pending item rather than a conversation.
- **No verified rule entitles you to evening classes.** The amendment is discretionary and administrative — which is precisely why the written request matters. A supervisor can only grant, deny, or answer something that formally exists; the filing date is what makes the agency's response time measurable and the review record coherent. The plan is judged as a whole at review — services, employment, and housing together — which is why repairing the plan beats sacrificing two of its goals to the third.

## 4. The traps

- **Quitting the job** (*Two Letters*, third choice → "Day Shift, No Shift"). The most tempting move in the scenario and the most expensive: attendance becomes perfect while the employment and housing goals collapse, reaching **"Ending — Everything Attended, Nothing Stable."** The judge's line says it all — the plan asked you to be in two places at once, and you chose the place with the chairs.
- **Asking the caseworker verbally** (*Two Letters*, second choice → "She Said She'd Ask"). Sympathy creates no pending item, no response deadline, no exhibit. Two weeks later nothing has moved, because nothing was ever formally asked.
- **Grinding without filing** (*Coffee and Alarms* → *The Third Note* → review). Three "fatigued" notes and a missed screen, in the vocabulary of refusal, with only your voice to explain them: **"Ending — Counted as Missed."** Note that filing late (from *The Third Note*) is strictly better than never filing — the judge acknowledges the late request and directs the agency to answer it — but it no longer reaches the win; the notes have already hardened.

## 5. What no strategy controls

Whether the provider runs an evening cohort at all is a funding-cycle fact, not a legal one. Dana's willingness to write the letter, a supervisor's response speed, and whether a judge presses the agency on a pending (rather than granted) request all vary by worker, courtroom, and luck. The published cases behind this scenario are full of parents who described impossible schedules in testimony — afterward, in the past tense. The route's only real claim is about tense and form: say it in the present, in writing, with the employer's letter attached, while the schedule can still be changed.
