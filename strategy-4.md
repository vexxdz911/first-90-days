# Strategy Guide — Scenario 4: “The Kinship Door”

*Game documentation for a fictional simulation. “In this scenario” means in the scenario’s data, debriefs, and sources file. This is general information, not legal advice for any real case.*

## 1. The win

There are two court-placement wins:

- **“Ending — Placement, by the Long Road”** — the recommended strategy win. The agency’s approval denial is upheld, but the timely written appeal carries the complete packet into Mia’s court file. At the December review, the court orders placement with her grandmother.
- **“Ending — The Court’s Different Question”** — the direct court win. The packet goes straight to the review and the court orders placement without the appeal detour. It arrives one simulated week earlier, but it leaves the approval appeal’s short clock running.

This guide chooses the long road as the strongest strategy, not because losing an appeal is good, but because it preserves the short-clock door and makes the eventual court record harder to reduce to one line. The placement is still partial in the human sense: it comes after months of visits, a denial, and a lost appeal—not in September when the grandmother first asked.

## 3. Why this wins — the law as it is supposed to work

- **The record request belongs to a party, and it opens the whole file.** In this scenario, the grandmother cannot request Mia’s case record as an applicant. Leah, the child’s mother and a party, can sign the request; under **55 Pa. Code § 3130.44(d)**, the material “shall be released upon request.” That distinction is the strategy’s hinge: ask the person with the right to use it.
- **The agency’s approval checklist and the court’s placement decision are different questions.** As the scenario and its sources file frame it, the criminal-history rule binds the agency’s approval authority; the checklist does not decide the dependency court’s broader placement question. The agency can truthfully say, “We cannot approve her,” without that sentence answering, “Where should this child live?”
- **A short-clock appeal should not be left to become a waiver.** The scenario deliberately gives no day-count for the approval appeal, so this guide claims none. The clinic’s rule is enough: file in writing and early, because a late appeal can become no appeal at all.
- **Losing the approval question can still build the court record.** The hearing officer applies the approval rule as written and upholds the denial. But the appeal assembles, stamps, and transmits the packet—the docket sheet, school-district letter, home study, personal statement, and 28 years of evidence—into the file the judge reads.
- **Discretion runs on what is in front of the decision-maker.** A one-line summary makes the grandmother look like a conviction with a date removed. The packet makes the court confront the sequence: 1998, the sentence, self-disclosure, decades of cleared work around children, a prepared home, and a child asking for that home.
- **Candor is cumulative.** The grandmother says the same thing to the study worker, the hearing officer, and the judge. Consistency lets the court treat the packet as a record rather than a performance prepared for one room.

## 4. The traps

- **Answering only the question asked about 1998** lets the old record enter without your own framing: what happened, that it did not involve a child, the sentence served, and the decades since. The history will be in the file either way; silence decides who narrates it.
- **Sparing Leah the record request** leaves you fighting from the one-line denial alone. Without the study, you cannot show the court the warm pages that the summary displaced.
- **Accepting “be the visit home” at Three Doors** closes the placement strategy and leads toward **“Ending — The Visit Home.”** It creates a valuable record, but it answers a request for presence when the filing asked for placement.
- **At the December Review, asking for visits at your home while the court considers the matter further** repeats that substitution at the final gate. The court is ready to decide placement; asking for a lesser, temporary arrangement leads to the visitation ending.
- **The court-first shortcut is a risk, not a loss.** Taking the packet straight to the review was graph-checked and reaches **“Ending — The Court’s Different Question.”** Do not mislabel it a trap. Its cost is strategic exposure: the approval appeal’s short clock continues running while you put everything on the court door.

## 5. What no strategy controls

The hearing officer cannot invent a time limit the approval rule does not contain. The agency’s lawyer can concede that the checklist does not bind the court, or resist longer. A judge can read the same packet and weigh 1998 more heavily than the following 28 years. Leah’s treatment, Mia’s advocate, and the review calendar also sit outside the grandmother’s control. The strategy controls candor, timing, collation, and persistence—the parts of discretion that belong to the family.
